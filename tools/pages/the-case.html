Chapter 1 — The engine

The sea that fed everyone

The Great Bear Sea — the coast from northern Vancouver Island to the Alaska border — covers more than 100,000 square kilometres of the most productive water in the North Pacific. Herring spawn by the billions. Salmon from dozens of rivers run through it. Whales follow the food.

For thousands of years it has been the economic engine and the food chain for the people who live here — First Nations first, then cannery towns, troll fleets, crab boats, charter operators. The order is simple: little fish feed big fish, and the sea feeds the coast.

Chapter 2 — The drag

Then the screen started to empty

Nine factory trawlers — most of them corporate-owned, several of them veterans of collapsed fisheries overseas — have swept roughly 89,700 square kilometres of BC's seafloor. That's an area bigger than Ireland, dragged through the very nurseries and feeding grounds that restock our fisheries.

The bycatch goes over the side. Under enhanced monitoring, groundfish trawlers caught nearly 30,000 salmon in a matter of months — 93% of them Chinook, the fish our endangered resident orcas starve without. And at-sea observers told investigators that intimidation kept an estimated 140 million pounds of bycatch off the books over two decades.

"It's akin to using a bulldozer to catch a butterfly. We wouldn't do this on land, so why do it in the oceans?" — Dr. Sylvia Earle, marine biologist

Chapter 3 — The stakes

When the fish go, the town goes

More than half of BC's assessed fish stocks are in trouble, and over 30 coastal species — salmon, herring, yelloweye rockfish among them — are listed at risk. This isn't an abstraction. It's the difference between a working harbour and a quiet one.

And the squeeze isn't coming from the family boat. Licences and quota now trade like stocks: an average groundfish-trawl licence package runs about $5.5 million, and one company alone holds 234 commercial licences on this coast. The wealth of the commons is being consolidated offshore of the communities that built it — while Ottawa is lobbied to open the North Coast to crude supertankers on top of it.

Chapter 5 — The plan

Draw the line. Let the sea do the work.

The Great Bear Sea MPA Network protects 30 percent of these waters — roughly 30,500 km² of nurseries, spawning grounds and feeding grounds — while the other 70 percent stays open to fishing. It was endorsed in 2023 by 15 First Nations, British Columbia and Canada after more than a decade of planning with harvesters and communities at the table. Bottom trawling is prohibited inside the Network as it's implemented, and Indigenous Guardians — already carrying park-ranger authority on parts of this coast — are on the water making protection real.

Here's the part opponents skip: protected cores are superchargers, not museums. Fish biomass inside well-managed MPAs measures ~670% higher; catch rates in the fishing grounds next door run up to 245% higher. Where open-net farms came off migration routes, coastal Nations and researchers are reporting stronger wild salmon returns. Protect the engine, and abundance carries past the line — into the nets of the boats that stayed.

And it's moving. In May 2026, six Nations — Wuikinuxv, Nuxalk, Kitasoo Xai'xais, Heiltsuk, Gitxaała and Gitga'at — signed the agreement with Canada and BC establishing Mia-yaltwa Ha'lidzogm hoon, the Central Coast's new National Marine Conservation Area Reserve. The name means "Realm of the Salmon, Home of the Salmon." This is not a proposal anymore. It's happening.

Chapter 6 — The ask

Hold the line. Finish the job.

Three jobs sit on desks in Ottawa and Victoria right now: implement the MPA Network on schedule, complete the fish-farm transition with no more extensions, and keep the North Coast tanker ban exactly where it is — Canada and BC pledged to uphold it in July 2026, and wins need defending. All three go better if the people of this coast are loud.
